5.1 Impact Flash Observations
The present-day cis-lunar environment is dynamic, with impacts from micrometeoroids, which are 
naturally occurring fragments of comets, asteroids, and planetary impact debris that travel through 
space. When these meteoroids strike the lunar surface, the kinetic energy of the impact creates seismic 
waves (Moser et al., 2015; Madiedo et al., 2019).
These impact events can be visible as a bright flash on the lunar surface and hence are observable from 
Earth and in cislunar space (Ortiz et al., 2000). In at least three instances, Apollo astronauts recorded 
observing impact flashes from orbit: one impact flash during Apollo 16 and two flashes during Apollo 
17. According to NASA’s Meteoroid Environment Office (MEO), at the time of this writing 443 impact 
flashes have been observed by Earth-based telescopes through a variety of observing campaigns, and 
NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) spacecraft has documented multiple new impact craters 
that have formed since its launch in 2009 (Moser et al., 2015; Sheward et al., 2022, 2025; Rizos et 
al., 2026).
Given previous observations of impact flashes during Apollo (NASA, 1972a, 1972b), plus ground-
based observations and LRO datasets (e.g., Suggs et al., 2014; Moser et al., 2015; Sheward et al., 2022, 
2025; Rizos et al., 2026), the LST strategically planned observation time for impact flash surveys, both 
by crew (Appendix 1) and by the Orion vehicle exterior cameras. More specifically, the crew had dedi-
cated time scheduled to observe impact flashes as a specific LTP target and during the eclipse period 
of the flyby. The eclipse period enabled observations at a time when the Sun was eclipsed behind the 
Moon. This provided dark viewing conditions with the Moon not directly illuminated by sunlight 
(only partially lit by earthshine) and removed any direct sunlight within the crew’s field of view (FOV), 
which aided with dark adaption for the crew’s eyes to adjust and be able to see fainter phenomena. 
Furthermore, the crew were trained to report any impact flashes observed at any point during the mis-
sion, even outside of scheduled surveys.
Additionally, a coordinated Earth-based observation campaign was also undertaken, including the 
MEO at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center through the Lunar Impact Flash Monitoring Program 
and complemented by an SMD citizen science campaign to increase public engagement and observing 
opportunities (Section 6.2). Earth-based observations were optimized to collect data near the times of 
the Artemis II crew observations, though this was not always possible given that ground-based observa-
tions require views of shadowed (non-illuminated) lunar terrain, which were not always present from 
the vantage point of Earth during the crew’s lunar flyby.
5.1.1 Characterizing Impact Events
Observations of impact flashes (or a lack thereof) during dedicated observation periods help 
characterize the modern impact environment and the scale of what may be observable from cis-lunar 
space. Of particular interest are constraining the meteoroid size and frequency distributions, as well as 
the timing, location, and brightness of individual flashes. The frequency distribution of impact flashes 
across the lunar surface can help constrain the impactor flux as a function of geographic location on 
the Moon (Ortiz et al., 2006). The frequency of flash observations, correlated with the presence (or 
absence) of known meteor showers during the observing periods, can provide insight into the sources 
and rates of the flashes (Madiedo et al., 2015; Ortiz et al., 2002). The timing and location of flashes

The Artemis II impact-flash observation campaign was designed to inform techniques for future 
observations and guide subsequent science investigations. For example, the statistics of impact-flash 
detections made by the crew, correlated with corresponding observing time, provide data on the detec-
tion frequency and probability for varying observing durations on future missions. These results can 
support the planning of observing surveys on crewed missions, as well as the selection of optimal detector 
systems for future flight missions. Additionally, the observing assets on Artemis II may constrain the 
expected frequency and magnitude of future impact flashes, thereby informing detection limits for 
future observations and highlighting the potential value of additional observing techniques, such as a 
dedicated automated flash-detection camera with increased sensitivity and/or resolution (Section 9.7). 
Overall, impact flash observations will contribute to future Artemis mission planning by improving 
understanding of the cis-lunar space environment and present-day meteoroid activity.
5.1.2 Impact Flash Observations
Five distinct impact flashes were reported by the crew during the period of the solar eclipse as seen 
from the vantage point of the Orion spacecraft. The crew possibly observed several additional impact 
flashes but did not formally report them. Crew members reported flashes in the crew PCD audio files, 
through O/E audio communication live during the flyby, and via an annotated image that was prepared 
by the crew and downlinked to the LST after the flyby on which the crew marked their approxima -
tion of where the five confirmed flashes were located on the lunar surface ( Figure 5.1). Several of the 
flashes were observed simultaneously by multiple crew members, cross validating the observations as 
true impact-flash detections.
All definitive flashes observed by the crew were seen during the lunar eclipse period of the lunar 
flyby, and no flashes were associated with any color. All the impact flashes were observed on the non-
illuminated portion of the Moon, and no flashes were reported in earthshine during the eclipse. No 
flashes were observed when any portion of the Moon was illuminated by the Sun; therefore, these 
flashes are likely the faintest ever observed by human eyes, suggesting small impactors on the order of 
several cm in diameter. Artemis II flew during a low meteoroid flux time of year outside of any major 
annual meteoroid streams, and thus these observations place a lower bound on the impact flux at the 
lunar surface.

Recommendation: Future Artemis crew training should develop flexible training modules that address 
just-in-time training needs for the crew and for science; build training opportunities into simulations, 
with scientists providing real-time feedback: assign geology mentors to individual crew members to

independently, providing frequent exposure to lunar science material with assessment and feedback 
delivered within one day to support knowledge retention.
9.4 In-Mission LST to Crew Communications
Real time, bidirectional communication between the LST and the crew proved to be a valuable asset 
throughout the mission, enabling the LST to respond to crew questions as they arose, request clarifi -
cation during time-critical science events, and directly support science interpretation while data were 
being collected.
Several times during the lunar flyby, the crew requested scientific information from the LST . For 
example, the crew asked for assistance with identifying surface features they were observing on the 
Moon in real time or determining relative ages of observed targets. Because the LST staffed in the SER 
had direct communication with the Science Officer, and because the Science Officer had direct com-
munication with the crew, these requests received immediate, in-mission responses.
During the solar eclipse period, the crew reported seeing several impact flashes. With direct voice 
communication, the LST was able to immediately solicit information on the timing and locations of 
the flashes, allowing the team to determine with greater confidence the number of flashes observed, 
which flashes were observed by multiple crew members, and where on the lunar surface the flashes were 
located. This additional data collection that was enabled through real-time communication between the 
LST and crew at the Moon proved critical for subsequent scientific analysis of these impact flash data.

9.6 Calibration of Cameras
Camera calibration will be essential for future missions to independently contextualize crew observa-
tions and enable quantitative analyses. Within the Artemis II mission architecture, the LST was not 
afforded the opportunity to select instrumentation or specify calibration needs in support of science. 
As discussed in Section 3.2.2, the cameras used during Artemis II were COTS systems with only partial 
pre-flight characterization, and no geometric, radiometric, or spectral calibration were completed on 
any of the included imaging systems.
T o enable quantitative use of images in future missions, it will be important to specify the minimum 
required calibration level needed to support science objectives. Camera characterization and calibra -
tion are needed to understand internal parameters (e.g., sensor response) and external factors (e.g., 
window effects; Section 7.2), as both influence image quality and the interpretation of color, bright -
ness, and shape. Additionally, the performance of manifested camera systems, including handheld, 
vehicle-mounted, and EVA tools, should be characterized in the unique lighting, thermal, and viewing 
environments in which they will be utilized.
Recommendation: Future Artemis missions should define minimum camera calibration require -
ments and characterize camera performance inside and outside the spacecraft to enable quantitative 
and independently verified crew observations.
9.7 Instrumentation for Enhanced Science Return
Artemis II highlighted several areas where dedicated science instrumentation would substantially 
improve the quality, interpretability, and diversity of lunar observations. Many of the lunar science 
objectives, particularly those associated with the characterization of impact flashes, color and albedo, 
photometry, dust detection, and deep-sky imaging, would have benefited from instruments specifically 
designed to address the unique observational challenges of cislunar space. Several illustrative examples 
of instruments for enhanced science return are outlined below. Missions to the surface of the Moon 
may have different instrument needs based on science objectives and the nature of in situ versus remote 
measurements (e.g., surface missions would benefit from strategically selected handheld field instru -
ments for crew members).
Although the crew observed and described several impact flashes during the solar eclipse, a dedi -
cated impact-flash sensor that is capable of high-frame-rate, high-dynamic-range imaging would allow 
systematic monitoring of impact events, enable quantitative flash brightness measurements, improve 
temporal precision and location identification, and enable direct comparison with human observations.
Calibrated and more capable imaging and/or spectrometer systems would improve collection of color 
and albedo data. The Artemis II crew frequently described subtle color variations across the lunar surface, 
but camera limitations prevented quantitative characterization. A calibrated multispectral camera and/
or dedicated spectrometer system(s) would provide independent measurements of spectral variations 
and allow direct comparison between crew perception and instrument-derived reflectance and spectral 
properties, strengthening scientific interpretation of surface composition and photometric behavior.
Relatedly, a physical onboard color wheel would provide the crew with a stable, standardized reference 
for color hue and value, improving consistency and reproducibility in real-time descriptions of lunar

For investigations of the lunar dust environment, future missions would benefit from a dedicated

Deep-sky imaging revealed optical constraints of general-purpose lenses. Coma and corner distortions

with human-driven observation workflows.
Recommendation: Future Artemis missions should manifest dedicated science instruments that

orbital observations similar to the Artemis II flyby observations, as well as EVA-specific instrumentation 
for use by astronauts on the lunar surface.
9.8 Window Design for Science Observations
For Artemis II, lunar images collected through Orion’s windows were a cornerstone of the lunar 
science dataset. These images enabled cross-regional comparisons, supported interpretation and con -
textualization of the crew’s verbal observations, and anchored the LST’s ability to map observations to 
specific lunar features. These images were also affected by the vehicle window design, including window 
size, pane design, and optical quality, and thus window design is a critical component in enabling high-
fidelity science observations.
The Artemis II window-quality DFTO results underscore the need to continue to characterize vehicle 
window designs. Windowpanes should be characterized individually and as an installed stack. This 
should be included for any updates to Orion’s windows that may be implemented for future Artemis 
flight missions and also the windows for the Human Landing System vehicles that will deliver subse -
quent Artemis crews to the lunar surface. Lower quality windows and/or incomplete characterization of 
windows for imaging will have a detrimental effect on the science return from human observations and 
images, particularly regarding phenomena that Artemis II demonstrated humans are adept at observing 
and describing, such as impact flashes, nuanced color patterns, and regional comparisons.
Recommendation: Future Artemis missions should rigorously characterize and prioritize window 
optical performance of windowpanes and the assembled window stack (for Orion and Human Landing 
System vehicles) to maximize the quality of science return from crew observations and images.
9.9 Crew-Audio Recording Capabilities and Time Synchronization of Multi-Modal 
Datasets
This report highlights the importance of crew observations to the mission’s science return. This 
highest-priority dataset was enabled on Artemis II via manual audio recordings captured by the crew 
on their PCDs. However, using PCDs for this critical scientific purpose introduced risks and challenges 
in several ways. First, the tablets were at significant risk of data loss due to radiation strikes. Without 
downlink capability, these critical data could have been permanently lost, as no backup solution existed. 
Second, each observation required the crew to manually start and stop the audio recordings on their 
PCDs. This added operational burden and increased the likelihood of missed activations or incomplete 
recordings, potentially compromising the dataset. Third, managing the PELTOR headsets, which were 
attached to the PCDs by lengthy cables floating in microgravity, proved cumbersome. By contrast, 
Apollo missions benefited from in-cabin recording devices that did not require any action from the

Further related to the value of audio recordings, the LST often relied on the sounds of camera shutter

missions, however, this approach will not always be viable. The sound of a Nikon D5 camera shutter or 
the simulated shutter sound of the Nikon Z9 will not reliably propagate to microphones embedded in 
EVA suits. Additionally, crew members on a surface EVA will not use PCDs for real-time annotations 
as IVA crews do, and will instead rely more heavily on verbal communication. As a result, correlating 
multi-modal science observations, including images, verbal descriptions, and other data streams during 
EVAs, will be even more dependent on precise and consistent timestamps across all devices.
Recommendation: Subsequent Artemis missions should incorporate a passive audio-recording capa-
bility to support the collection of crew observations. A continuous, timestamped onboard audio record 
would also provide an independent means of establishing the timing of activities not captured by other 
onboard systems, substantially improving post-mission reconstruction and interpretation of the sequence 
of mission events. This capability would be particularly beneficial during highly dynamic science mis-
sion phases, such as the Artemis II lunar flyby. Passive audio recording would reduce crew workload 
by eliminating the need to manage additional hardware during high-priority and time-critical periods. 
Additionally, passive audio would capture valuable scientific context by recording crew perception dur-
ing mission phases where opportunistic or serendipitous science data is collected. This recommendation 
aligns with crew feedback in the crew perspectives section (Section 2.0).
Recommendation: Subsequent Artemis missions should maintain coordinated and correct timestamp 
synchronization across all science-relevant systems, including cameras, crew suits, PCDs, voice loops, 
any additional manifested science payloads, and the spacecraft, to ensure accurate alignment of multi-
modal datasets during orbital and surface mission phases.
