Artemis II Lunar Science Data User Guide 
1. Overview 
1.1. Purpose and Scope of Document 
This Lunar Science Data User Guide (“User Guide”) describes the data products in the Artemis II science 
data archive in sufficient detail to enable users to read and understand the data. It also describes the 
Planetary Data System (PDS) archive bundle s, which define the structure in which the data products, 
documentation, and supporting materials are stored. This User Guide is intended for the scientists who will 
analyze the data, including those associated with the project , members of the general planetary science 
community, and users from the broader public who w ould like a deeper understanding of the data. 
Data products described in this User Guide are science observations curated by the Artemis II Lunar Science 
Team (LST), including images of the Earth, Moon and deep space collected by the Artemis II crew using 
handheld cameras, crew-recorded verbal observations of the Moon, Orion vehicle camera imagery collected 
during lunar flyby, temperature and humidity data of Orion sample storage lockers, and other opportunistic 
science observations of the Earth-Moon system. These products and archive were guided by the Artemis II 
Open Science and Data Management Plan (OSDMP) developed by the LST prior to the mission . There are 
many more image and data products collected during the Artemis II mission that were not curated by the 
LST but may be of interest to users seeking a broader and more contextual understanding of mission events. 
Although not included in this science archive, r eferences and lin ks to other science or non- science data 
from the mission are provided in Section 1.5. 
Note: At the time of writing, the archive collection for the small number of crew captured images from the 
iPhone 17 Pro Max was still being prepared. There are references to these images throughout this User 
Guide; however, an update to t he archive and th is User Guide will be made upon later release.

1.3. Getting Started 
Section 1 provides an overview of this document and quick references for getting familiar with the archive. 
Section 2 provides an overview of the Artemis II mission , its science objectives , and major science 
observations conducted during the mission . Major activit ies are broken down by mission flight day and 
time range. Section 3 provides descriptions of the cameras and other instruments used to record these 
observations, including information on observing sequences and the configuration and settings used during 
the observations. Section 4 provides a description of the PDS4 archive structure and the types of products 
included in the archive . Section 5 describes the processes and software used to generate data products , 
including the important information required to understand and use the data. 
1.3.1. Accessing and searching the archive 
As described in Section 4, Artemis II science data bundles are archived in the Planetary Data System (PDS) 
at different Discipline Nodes according to their area of expertise. For this archive, the PDS Geosciences 
(GEO) Node is the Lead Node and hosts the mission bundle of documents [including the Data User Guide, 
OSDMP, static Lunar Targeting Packag e ( LTP), and Outgoing Target Request (OTR) list] and Orion 
environment data (data logger temperature and humidity data), the Cartography and Imaging Sciences (CIS) 
Node hosts crew handheld camera and Orion camera image and audio bundles , and the Navigation and 
Ancillary Information Facility (NAIF) Node hosts the SPICE bundle of kernels with spacecraft and 
instrument navigation and ancillary information (e.g., trajectory, attitude, etc.). Each node provides public 
access to the data via its website and the main PDS website provides a search function across all of PDS. 
Table 1 lists the recommended citation and Uniform Resource Locators (URLs) for the main PDS search 
function and for the specific websites for each bundle at the various PDS nodes. Note that these URLs are 
not immutable and may change in the future . 
The PDS provides specific web-based tools that allow for facet -based search on the metadata (e.g., time of 
observation, camera type, processing level, exposure, etc .). For Artemis II science image data, the PDS 
Cartography and Imaging Sciences Node provides search capabilities through its Image Atlas: https://pds-
imaging.jpl.nasa.gov/tools/atlas/search 
Users preferring to browse the web- based directory structure can do so by starting at the parent directory 
for each data bundle and then navigating down into collections and subdirectories . At the CIS Node, 
browsing can be completed using the Atlas Archive Explorer: 
https://pds-imaging.jpl.nasa.gov/tools/atlas/archive-explorer 
Other starting points for directory browsing are found at the websites listed in Table 1. For advanced users 
wanting programmatic access to the archive, PDS also provides an API that can be used to search PDS4 
data included as part of the registry : https://nasa-pds.github.io/pds-api/index.html 
Users can always contact the Planetary Data System for assistance finding a data product: 
• General PDS inquiries: pds_operator@jpl.nasa.gov 
• Geosciences Node: geosci@wunder.wustl.edu 
• Cartography and Imaging Sciences Node: pds-img-jpl@jpl.nasa.gov or pds-img-usgs@usgs.gov

bundle and collection metadata labels.

2. Artemis II Mission Overview 
NASA’s Artemis II mission was primarily a human spaceflight test mission to demonstrate that the 
integrated Space Launch System (SLS) rocket and Artemis II Orion spacecraft could safely support human 
life in the deep -space environment beyond low -Earth orbit. The mission had four crew members: 
Commander (CDR) Reid Wiseman, Pilot (PLT) Victor Glover, and Mission Specialist (MS1) Christina 
Koch from NASA and Mission Specialist (MS2) Jeremy Hansen from the Canadian Space Agency (CSA).

2.2. Mission Events and Science Activities and Instruments 
A summary of the major mission events and science observing time periods for each flight day (FD) is 
provided in Table 4. The Artemis II mission began on Flight Day 1 ( 2026-04-01 22:35:12 UTC) with a 
successful launch of the Space Launch System (SLS) rocket and a proximity operations demonstration 
where the crew manually maneuvered Orion near the spent Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) 
rocket stage. On Flight Day 2, the spacecraft executed the critical Translunar Injection (TLI) burn, sending 
the cre w toward the Moon. Flight Day 3 was marked by the cancellation of the first outbound trajectory 
correction (OTC) burn due to the precision of the TLI, allowing the crew to focus on medical drills and 
cabin setup. Flight Day 4 featured a manual piloting demonstration to test Orion’s handling in deep space, 
while Flight Day 5 involved a comprehensive evaluation of the Orion Crew Survival System suits and the 
OTC-2 burn. The outbound phase culminated on Flight Day 6 with the lunar flyby, during which the crew 
conducted the mission’s primary science observation s and passed approximately 6,543 km above the lunar 
farside at closest approach (i.e., lunar pericynthion) at 2026-04-06 23:00 UTC. 
The return journey commenced on Flight Day 7 with the first return trajectory correction burn (RTC-1) and 
the initial public release of captured images from lunar flyby. On Flight Day 8, the crew conducted a 
radiation shielding demonstration and held a live news conference from deep space, followed by Flight Day 
9, which was dedicated to the RTC- 2 burn and final cabin stowage for reentry. The mission concluded on 
Flight Day 10 as Orion performed a final trajectory burn and a skip reentry maneuver to shed velocity. The 
spacecraft splashed down safely in the Pacific Ocean on at 2026-04-11 00:07 UTC. 
A number of NASA and public websites can be searched to provide more complete summaries of mission 
events, including: 
• NASA News Release: https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/ 
• NASA Artemis Blogs: https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/artemis/ 
As Table 4 summarizes, Artemis II science observation activities spanned the mission with Flight Days 1-
5 involving short ~15-30 min opportunistic imaging events when Orion windows were purposefully pointed 
at the Earth or Moon , a window quality assessment, and final mission preparation for the lunar flyby 
observations. Primary lunar observations occurred during an approximately 6.75- hour period around lunar 
flyby on Flight Day 6 . The observing sequence during lunar flyby was structured around the Lunar 
Targeting Plan (LTP), which is described in more detail (with UTC times) in the Lunar Science Operations 
Report and Preliminary Lunar Science Report, with a static pdf version of the LTP provide d as a document 
to this archive. Flight Day 7 was dedicated to final observations and crew debrief activities . Post-
splashdown activities included a dedicated lunar observation debrief with the crew. Serendipitous science 
observations were made throughout the 10-day mission, when the Earth, the Moon, or the Milky Way were 
visible to the crew from the Orion windows, whether observation -enabling spacecraft pointing was 
intentional for science or not. Table 4 lists these “un scheduled” crew science activities, with time ranges 
determined based on the beginning and end of clusters of crew camera image collection times.

3. Instrument and Sensor Overview and Observation s 
This section provides an overview of the cameras and other instruments used to conduct scientific 
observations and to collect the measurements and data contained in this archive. Details on camera and 
instrument specifications are provided in the Artemis II OSDMP, Section 5. Science Instrumentation 
and references therein which is included as a document to t his archive. 
3.1. Crew Observations: Nikon D5 and Z9 Cameras 
Two handheld Nikon D5 digital single -lens reflex (DSLR) cameras (Serial Numbers , SN, 3500015 and 
3500017) were used by the crew during the Artemis II mission. One camera (SN 3500015) was primarily 
paired with an 80 -400 mm focal length “Long Lens ” for about 98% of its science images and the other 
camera (SN 3500017) was primarily paired with the 12-24 mm “Short Lens” for about 57% of its science 
images ( and 29% with the Long Lens) during opportunistic imaging and lunar flyby imaging activities 
(Table 4, Figure 1). The cameras could also be paired with a 35- mm lens for deep space imaging (occurred 
in < 15% of science images) , but this lens was intended for use for interior images . Images were collected 
with the cameras pointing out various Orion windows depending on the observation activity and crew 
configuration (Section 3.1.1). As a mirrored camera, the viewfinder of the Nikon D5 was used by the crew 
as a monocle for conducting observations of the Moon and other targets of interest. The mirrored viewfinder 
allowed the crew’s eyes to stay adjusted to the lunar surface brightness while making descriptions at a 
higher magnification. Due to its proven reliability and ability to withstand the ionizing radiation and thermal 
stresses of low Earth orbit, NASA has integrated unmodified Nikon D5 units into its standard flight kit for 
the International Space Station (ISS) and astronaut training . For Artemis II, the history of the camera on 
the ISS as well as the extremely low light sensitivity made the Nikon D5 ideal for lunar observations. 
One handheld Nikon Z9 camera (SN 39200019) was used for science observations throughout the mission . 
It was manifested to provide a complementary dataset to the Nikon D5 images and to test data collection 
procedures in prepa ration for future Artemis missions where the Z9 will be the primary camera as part of 
the technical core of the Handheld Universal Lunar Camera (HULC). During Artemis II, the Nikon Z9 was 
primarily paired with a 35-mm lens for 74% of its science images (other images were most ly with the Long 
Lens) and was the primary camera used during the eclipse observations conducted during the l unar flyby.

Image Sequence During the lunar flyby, the image sequence was generally driven by the Lunar Targeting 
Plan, but the crew had flexibility to image other targets during the observation period . 
The crew captured multiple images over a single target area to enable advanced post-
processing, such as the generation of High Dynamic Range (HDR) products. Crew 
members were trained to first take context images with a zoomed-out view before using 
the zoom capabilities. For large areas, crew members were trained to take mosaics 
with a 30 -50% overlap. Image sequences also include multiple image captures at 
different camera settings as the crew made adjustments to achieve the desired image 
quality. 
3.1.2. Window Quality DFTO and observing windows 
The Artemis II Orion forward windows (Figure 2) are made of a three-layer stack of 0.5-inch-thick panes, 
with an outer thermal pane of fused silica , a middle thermal pane of cast acrylic and inner pressure pane of 
stretched acrylic These layers, especially the cast acrylic can distort and alter images collected through 
them. Unfortunately, pre-mission characterization of images collected using the Nikon cameras and through 
the full three-stack panes of the forward windows on Orion could not be conducted. It was therefore critical 
to assess window quality in flight but before the lunar flyby science observations to determine if a change 
in observing configuration was necessary . To assist in the assessment, on Flight Day 3 Pilot Victor Glover 
performed a Window Quality Developmental Flight Test Objective (DFTO) to assess window quality for 
observations. Table 4 provides the UTC time range of observations made during the DFTO activities. 
A report on the Window Quality DFTO analysis and results is provided in Appendix E. Based on the results, 
no changes to the planned configuration for flyby observations were made. The analysis revealed that 
Window 2 was the best window for imaging and that the crew had a GO for collecting images at 400 mm 
focal lengths. The spacecraft was pointed to position the Moon in Window 2 with the recommendation to 
conduct imaging observations from the left position (left side of the window when facing the window from 
inside the cabin). This was communicated via a Flight Note and verbalized over the Orion-to-Earth 1 voice 
loop as a reminder during the pre -flyby preparations on Flight Day 6.

3.2. Mission Audio: PCD and Voice Loops 
3.2.1. PCD audio descriptions 
Crew members used four Portable Computing Devices (PCDs) during the mission, with one (PCD 4) failing 
part-way through the mission. Their primary uses for science observations were 1) to record audio of crew 
observations while paired with a Peltor Headset, 2) to record handwritten crew notes of observations, 3) to 
serve as an imagery relay for mission downlink, and 4) to provide the Lunar Targeting Package to support 
lunar observations. 
To start the mission , each crew member had an assigned PCD , which was a Microsoft Surface Pro 5 tablet

recording audio observations (using the system audio recorder and Peltor headset) from crew members 
configured at Orion ’s Windows 2 and 3. Throughout the mission , e ach PCD was synced with the Orion 
clock time, which also served as the reference clock for the crew handheld cameras. The audio recordings 
were saved as M4A formatted files and downlinked during the mission for use in scientific analysis (Section 
5.4). 
Verbal observations were made by the crew with both the unaided eye and with the Nikon D5 camera 
(configuration described more in Section 3.1.1 and Table 5). The crew noted during the post-mission debrief 
that during audio recordings , the Peltor headset was often worn in a one- ear on, one -ear off configuration 
to allow the crew member to be able to converse with each other about synchronous observations. These 
conversations are apparent in the PCD audio recordings where multiple crew members can be heard in the

3.3.4. Orion DCAM and OpNav imaging activities 
The DCAM primarily supported the Proximity Operations (Prox Ops) demonstration about 3 hours into the 
mission. During Prox Ops the DCAM was used to visualize the detached upper stage of the SLS as the crew 
piloted Orion to simulate rendezvous with a future lunar lander. The OpNav camera takes images of 
celestial bodies, like the Earth and Moon, against starfield backgrounds. By measuring the size, position, 
and phase of these objects, the onboard camera computes the spacecraft's exact range, bearing, and 
trajectory (Inman et al 2024). Since the DCAM and OpNav cameras are fixed to the spacecraft , the attitude 
and trajectory determine the field of view of the camera. Following the successful Prox Ops demonstration , 
the DCAM as well as the OpNav cameras collected additional imaging of both the Earth and Moon when 
they were in their view or in support of other mission or Developmental Flight Test Objectives (DFTOs). 
Though these images were not planned by the science team, they are of secondary benefit and could be 
used for future scientific analyses .

4.2. Archive Bundles, Collections, and Products 
PDS develops standards for stable, accessible, well -documented science archives, and specifies how these 
standards are implemented. The current standard for PDS archives is PDS4. This standard organizes science 
data and documentation into bundles, collections, and products, summarized below. Readers unfamiliar 
with the PDS4 standard are referred to the PDS Concepts Document ( listed in Section 1.2) for a detailed 
explanation. 
The highest level of organization for a PDS 4 archive is the bundle. A bundle is a set of one or more related 
collections which may be of different types. A collection is a set of one or more related basic products that 
are typically all of the same type. Bundles and collections are logical structures, not necessarily tied to any 
physical directory structure or organization. 
The Artemis II science data archive is organized into five PDS4 bundles by instrument platform (crew and 
Orion camera bundles), mission (mission audio and mission document bundles), or navigation and ancillary 
information (SPICE bundle) . The Artemis II science data bundles include collections of data products, 
documents, and other products organized by processing level and type, as shown in Table 10. 
Within each collection, individual products are generally arranged into sub-directories by Flight Day (fd01, 
fd02, fd03, etc.). Placement into Flight Day subdirectories was determined based on the metadata UTC date 
and time recorded by each instrument or sensor at the time of collection. The relationship between Flight 
Day and UTC time is provided in Table 4. Each product also has its own unique name based on the file 
naming conventions in Appendix D. More details on product types are provided in Section 5 , with bundle 
and collection directory structure provided in more detail in Appendix C. 
Each data product consists of one digital object in one file, accompanied by a detached PDS4 label file in 
XML format (Section 4.3). Document files are also archived and assigned PDS labels. PDS labels provide 
identification and other metadata for the data and document files. Collections and bundles are also 
considered products in PDS 4 and therefore have their own labels. 
Every product, including collections and bundles , also has a unique logical identifier (LID) . Each LID 
begins with the prefix “urn:nasa:pds”. The LIDs for b undle and collection s in the archive are shown in 
Table 10. Product LIDs are composed of the collection LID plus a colon plus the product label file name, 
minus the XML extension and version number. An example LID for a Nikon D5 source product would be : 
urn:nasa:pds:artemis2_crew_camera:data_source:art002eXXXXXX_nkd5015_src.nef

The metadata for a PDS4 product is recorded in a PDS4 label using XML (eXtensible Markup Language). 
Typically, a label has the same file name as the data file it describes but with the extension “.xml”. A 
product may consist of more than one data object (i.e. a table or an image) in a file, and it may consist of 
more than one data file, but a product always has ex actly one detached PDS4 label file. Some data products 
have other kinds of metadata embedded in the data files. XML is a widely used standard for storing 
metadata, and while it is easy for software to read, it can be more difficult for humans to read. XML labels 
are best viewed using an XML -aware editor that can help by color- coding the XML elements and allowing 
the reader to open and close sections of the label. Some commonly used XML -aware editors include 
Notepad++, Visual Studio Code, xmplify, and Oxygen. Oxygen is the editor used by many PDS personnel.

5.1. General Archive Generation Pipeline 
Source data and metadata were received by the Lunar Science Team and then processed via a PDS4 archive 
software pipeline developed in close collaboration with the PDS. The basic steps used to generate the data -
archive products, collections, and bundles are summarized below : 
1. Generate bundle directory structure (Bundle/Collections/Subfolders) 
2. Generate products by processing source data and other higher- level data products according to the 
steps described in Sections 5.2 to 5.4. 
3. Design PDS4 XML labels using sample data products from pre-mission and mission files : 
a. The Lunar Science Team and the PDS reviewed the embedded metadata for each data type 
and determined which subset of the metadata provided scientifically useful and accurate 
information for inclusion in the label. The final subset of metadata was chosen to provide 
an information-rich label while minimizing information on instrument settings or attributes 
that was either redundant , poorly understood, or was not used during the mission. Label 
templates were designed that mapped the chosen subset of embedded metadata tags to 
existing PDS4 Data Dictionary class es and attributes or , in some cases, to newly 
development Data Dictionary classes or attributes. 
4. For each data product , generate PDS4 XML labels: 
a. Extract embedded metadata from source data (using ExifTool, or other metadata extraction 
tool; see Section 6) into a json file and append additional metadata structures as needed. 
b. Use the PDS-provided software (Labelocity, see Section 6) and label template mapping to 
the PDS4 Data Dictionaries with json metadata file to generate XML PDS4 label files for 
each data product. 
5. Assemble data products and labels into bundles and collections. 
6. Generate collection product inventory and XML label file for each collection (one inventory file 
and one label file for each collection) . 
7. Generate label files for each bundle (one per bundle) 
8. Validate each Bundle using PDS 4 Validate tool. 
a. Generate a validation report . 
b. Iterate as necessary by resolving validation issues and re -running the Validate Tool until 
the issues resolve. 
5.2. Crew Camera Data Products

converted to PDS4- compliant array image format, processed images, and browse image products. No

5.2.1. Nikon sensor color array and image processing 
The Sun emits energy over a broad spectrum of wavelengths ( Figure 5a), with visible light representing a 
narrow band of reflected energy over the range of approximately 380- 750 nm, near the peak of emission of 
solar energy. Humans perceive color as the light that is reflected, with the Earth’s atmosphere absorbing a 
significant fraction of the total solar radiation energy (Figure 5a). Sensors for light – from human eyes to 
the handheld cameras designed to record that light – are tailored for the solar energy that makes it through 
Earth’s atmosphere. Outside of Earth, the entire solar spectrum becomes available again and can be altered 
in a similar way by gases or particle s encountered elsewhere.

The sensors of both the Nikon D5 and Z9 cameras were not specifically designed for space or lunar 
observations and only record a part of the total reflectance from incoming solar radiation over the 400 -700 
nm color spectrum of visible light. Although the specifications are unpublished by Nikon, the company’s 
cameras have proprietary filters that allow light to pass through three narrow bandwidths of the visible 
spectrum at red (R), green (G) or blue (B) wavelengths, arranged in a Bayer mosaic color filter array (Figure 
5b,c). In this arrangement, there are twice the number of green pixels on the sensor – half of the total – with 
the remaining half split evenly between red and blue. This pattern has alternating rows of BGBGBGBGB 
and GRGRGRGRG with the green pixels arranged diagonally from one another. The shorthand for this 
type of color filter array (CFA) is RGGB (Figure 5b ). 
The RGGB Bayer pattern is recorded and stored differently in the different products included in this archive: 
1. Source Data Products (Section 5.2.2): The recorded Nikon Electronic Format (NEF) wraps and 
reversibly compresses the Bayer pattern in a proprietary format. Nikon has not published details 
on the specifications for this format, so the RGB filter parameters are not fully known. 
2. Raw Data Products (Section 5.2.3): For scientific use and for long -term preservation, the raw 
values from each of the RGB sensors were extracted from the NEF files, leaving the Bayer pattern 
intact but as a single grayscale image . The open-source software Libraw was used for this 
processing. When zooming in on these images they will appear pixelated and show the RGGB 
Bayer pattern in grayscale (Figure 6). This reduced file-size approach results in each pixel 
recording only a single color channel (R, G, or B). There were no additional sensors on this mission 
to validate the color spectrum retrieved by the Nikon cameras or to perform calibration. 
3. Processed Data Products (Section 5.2.4): The Bayer pattern can be “demosaiced” using a post-
processing algorithm that interpolates the full RGB color o ver every pixel given the retrieved 
RGGB values. The Nikon NX Studio software was used for the generation of processed products, 
as its algorithm provides the best -tuned interpretation of the proprietary format for RGB 
interpolation. For processed images, different software interprets and processes the Bayer pattern 
and camera metadata with varying results. Each of these demosaicing algorithm s has the potential 
to introduce artifacts as they w ere trained on images attained of Earth where the atmosphere has 
removed a significant fraction of insolation ( Figure 5a, the light gray shading). Open-source tools 
that apply algorithms to infer the RGB of each pixel often create spurious artifacts, or “mealworm” 
looking images on close inspectio n. Images processed through NX Studio typically avoid these 
artifacts but may reduce fine details .

5.2.2. Crew camera: Source data products 
The source data products are the original Nikon image files recorded by the cameras, with no processing

Libraw maintains an emulator for dcraw as well as several other functions to retrieve both the original Bayer

Unfortunately, as of the preparation of this archive, the Libraw library and other available open- source 
software did not support the direct processing of the Nikon Z9 NEF-HE* format to a product that preserves 
the Bayer pattern. As a solution, processing of Nikon Z9 NEF-HE* images were completed by first

interface. After, the DNG files we re produced, the 16 -bit TIFF with the Bayer pattern intact was created

option can automatically provide autoscaling to compensate. 
5.2.4. Crew camera: Processed data products 
Processed products were generated to provide uncalibrated but interpreted image pr oducts that have 
undergone the most basic processing . They were generated by converting from the native source NEF or 
NEF-HE* format (.nef) to uncompressed 16 -bit TIFF format (.tif) using the vendor- provided Nikon NX 
Studio software, NX Studio (2021) version 1.10.1 (see Section 6, Table 21) This software is available by 
Nikon for free with some limitations for use with its cameras and NEF digital negative images . NX Studio 
software algorithms are proprietary with a finely tuned methodology for demosaicing the RGGB Bayer 
pattern recorded by its sensors to a final, visually finished product. 
In batch, images were exported as uncompressed 16-bit TIFF files with demosaicing using default Original 
Camera Settings (determined from the recorded camera metadata) and with sRGB and autoscaling applied 
to ensure that the images are viewable when expanded to 16 -bit. Screenshots of the settings used are 
provided in Appendix F. NX Studio produces two images within the 16 -bit TIFF output, the large, full 
resolution image as well as a browse image used by modern GUI based file browsers. As the processing 
algorithm employed by the software is likely based on images taken on Earth, it is possible that spurious 
artifacts could be introduced when applied to space -based and lunar observations . Colors output by the 
process cannot be completely verified or quantified w ithout further information, calibration, and testing. 
Other demosaicing software can be used to generate similar outputs to NX Studio but with varying results. 
For example, Adobe products (e.g., Photoshop) use Adobe Camera Raw software which can produce a near 
facsimile of the NX Studio postprocessing. The JPEG images available online at Gateway to Astronaut

5.2.6. PDS4 labels and metadata 
PDS4 labels were generated from a combination of the embedded metadata and additional metadata 
provided by the Lunar Science Team according to the following process (and outlined in Section 5.1) : 
1. Extract embedded metadata from Nikon Raw or Nikon Raw HE* .nef file as json. 
2. Add additional science team provided metadata fields to the json files (Table 17). 
3. Using internal and PDS-provided label template scripts with the image and json pairs, generate 
XML label file. 
The source NEF data products contain numerous embedded metadata fields (or “tags”) that describe 
information about the camera settings and parameters used to capture the image in standard formats. The 
open source ExifTool software (https://exiftool.org/; Harvey, 2026) was used to extract and output these 
metadata fields for processing of PDS4 labels. Other open- source tools are also available to view and 
manage Nikon camera metadata (see Section 6 and Table 21). ExifTool allows for reading and the 
extraction of various groups of metadata tags, including: 
• Exchangeable image file format (Exif) (CIPA DC -008-Translation-2026 Exchangeable image file 
format for digital still cameras: Exif Version 3.1, https://www.cipa.jp/e/std/history_sec.html) 
• XMP "Extensible Metadata Platform" 
• Camera specific tags from Nikon and GoPro 
• Alternate language tags, e.g., in XMP, PNG, QuickTime, and MXF information

5.2.7. Geolocation of images and control points 
The PDS4 metadata labels for processed products include control points and corner coordinates for 3 ,687 
Nikon images of the Moon . A visualization of these image footprints binned by focal length is shown in 
Figure 7, where the footprint curvature is due to geolocation around the lunar limb and illuminated portions. 
The ground control points were produced following the strategy for geolocating astronaut photography of 
Earth developed in Find My Astronaut Photo (Stoken, 2023). Specifically, an illumination adjusted (for the 
time of the flyby) basemap was produced, tiled, and then embedded using the AstroLoc (Berton et al., 2025)

Following EarthMatch (Berton et al ., 2024) and using the LoMa -R (Nordström, 2026) image matching 
model as implemented in vismatch, the crew photo is matched to retrieved tiles (in the order of lowest to 
highest embedding distance). Once a tile has over N inlier matches, it is considered a positive pair and the 
matches become control points, connecting pixel coordinates in the crew image to points in the reference 
tile (which have known geocoordinates). Geocoordinates use the Moon IAU 2000 coordinate system for a 
spheroid with a semi -major axis radius of 1737. 4 km and flattening of 0 . Using the RANSAC algorithm, 
an affine transform is fit to the control points with rasterio (GDAL python wrapper). Then, corner 
coordinates are extracted from the resulting affine transform. For computational efficiency, t iepoints are 
computed on resized images (518x518 pixels for both crew image and retrieved tile) and then the tie points 
are rescaled to the full image resolution using (image_width/ 518, image_height/518) as the scaling factor . 
Each row/col value is scaled by the factor corresponding to its axis. Thus, there is inherent tiepoint precision 
loss due to matching on the downsampled image . 
Given the limits of fitting the affine transform to a spheroid, for imagery with significant curvature the 
resulting transforms are only accurate in portions of the image . For images with only some corners 
intersecting the Moon, the non -intersecting corners are not defined. In some cases, typically when the full 
lunar disk is visible, there are valid control points but an invalid footprint (with image corner coordinates 
outside valid latitude/longitude ranges) due to the affine transform assumption and the image extent 
expanding off-Moon. When this occurs, footprint vertices as well as horizontal/vertical pixel field of view 
(derived from vertices) are omitted in the image metadata label , but control points are retained in the label 
for users who may want to use other transform methods . 
Footprint coordinates are provided to assist users in spatial analysis of the data. Raw control points are 
provided for those users who would like to apply custom transforms for georectification, or other custom 
adjustments.

The ground-recorded MXF video files use a codec that is not PDS4 compliant, and these files were therefore 
converted to MP4 (.mp4) with H.264 codec using the open -source software FFMPEG version N-121682-
g7435a3180d (2025). Conversion from MXF to MP4 was achieved using the following command: 
ffmpeg -i ${f} -map 0:v:0 -map 0:a -c:v libx264 -crf 10 -preset slow -profile:v 
high -pix_fmt yuv420p -c:a aac -b:a 256k -fps_mode cfr -map_chapters 0 - f mp4 
${new_rawfname} 
FFMPEG is a command line only interface. HandBrake is another open source tool that uses the FFMPEG 
libraries but also has a graphical user interface. The equivalent conversion from MXF to MP4 using the 
HandBrakeCLI 1.11.2 (2026) command is: 
HandBrakeCLI -i ${f} -o ${new_rawfname} -e x264 -q 10 --encoder- preset slow -
-encoder-profile high --crop 0:0:0:0 --keep- display-aspect --all- audio --
aencoder aac --ab 256 --markers --format av_mp4 --cfr 
Further, SAW Cam 3 video was recorded at the NASA JSC video handling facility on two separate 
Downlink Decoders (or channels). Both videos are essentially identical during lunar flyby but are preserved 
in the archive in separate sub -directories (channel1 and channel2). 
5.3.4. Orion camera: Metadata n otes 
Corrections were made to the embedded times for original OpNav and DCAM images. For OpNav images, 
the embedded CreateDate time is related to the downlink time. The actual recorded time was contained in 
the original filename (housed inside of the Exiftool -XMP-iptcCore:AltTextAccessibility tag), which 
was extracted and re -constructed for inclusion of a corrected time in the PDS4 label metadata. For DCAM 
images, only a best estimate of the collection time based on received image directory batch times could be 
made. These corrected times were cross-checked with scheduled mission activities but may be offset by up 
to 5 minutes or more from the actual collection time.

5.4.1. Mission audio: Audio products 
The PCD audio recorder natively recorded as MPEG-4 Audio (M4A, or .m4a) files using the AAC

recordings were generated by the crew for each target in the Lunar Targeting Plan (LTP), although often a 
single file contains descriptions of multiple targets.

5.4.2. Mission audio: Transcripts 
Transcripts for PCD and voice loop audio recordings were generated using the open -source package 
whisperX (https://github.com/m-bain/whisperx), which is an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model 
originally developed by OpenAI. Transcripts were generated using the large-v2 model with 
compute_type=float16 followed by an alignment model to generate per -word timestamps. Following 
transcription, additional UTC and Mission Elapsed Time (MET) information was added via custom Lunar 
Science Team developed scripts based on the audio creation time metadata stored in the audio file itself. 
Following automatic generation, transcripts were manually checked by the Lunar Science Team and, as 
needed, corrected. Common corrections included misspellings of lunar feature or crew member names. 
Speaker diarization was also added manually during this p rocess. Corrections generally followed the 
guidelines established by the Smithsonian Digital Volunteers Transcription Center , 
(https://transcription.si.edu/instructions/specialized/audio) with minor modifications made to tailor to the 
Artemis II mission audio files. 
One transcript file was produced for each PCD audio (.m4a) file. Voice Loop Transcripts were provided 
and edited for the UTC days of lunar flyby (2026 -04-06 and 2026-04-07) only.

5.6.2. Outgoing Target Request (OTR) 
The Outgoing Target Request (OTR) is a json file generated by the Artemis II Lunar Science Team in real -
time during the mission and delivered to the Crew Lunar Observations (CLO) sub team for processing into 
the Lunar Targeting Plan. The file details in plaintext the specifics of the science team’s request as well as 
information about a target’s observation availability window, relationship to the Science Traceability 
Matrix (STM), and metadata required for LTP generation. A static view of the OTR is included as a json 
file to the document collection of this archive.

7.4. Appendix D. File Naming Convention 
Every PDS4 Archive Data Product produced by the mission that has an assigned NASA ID will have a file 
name that follows the convention specified below. The primary goals of this naming convention are: 
• Ensure uniqueness for every single archive data product across all instruments, all venues, and for 
the life of the mission. 
• Provide consistency in structure to ease parsing of the various fields 
• Provide both machine and human readability of file names. 
In section 6C.1.1 of the PDS Standards Reference, the PDS identifies the rules governing selection of file 
names. The following subset of those rules pertains to this convention: 
• The file name must be unique within a directory. 
• The file name must be no longer than 255 characters. 
• The file name must be case -insensitive. 
• The file name must be constructed from the character set 
• A-Z ASCII 0x41 through 0x5A, 
• a-z ASCII 0x61 through 0x7A, 
• 0-9 ASCII 0x30 through 0x39, 
• dash “-” ASCII 0x2D 
• underscore “_” ASCII 0x5F and 
• period “.” ASCII 0x2E 
• The file name must not begin or end with a dash, underscore, or period 
• The file name must include at least one period followed by an extension 
The fields that make up the file name are depicted as follows (for data with NASA IDs): 
<NASA ID>_<instrument ID>_<product type>_<version>.<extension>

7.5. Appendix E. Window Quality DFTO Report 
The following is the report prepared by NASA JSC ISAG and the Artemis II Lunar Science Team after the 
FD03 Window Quality DFTO to record the initial image assessments and recommendations on window 
location and camera placement for the lunar flyby. 
7.5.1. Background of activity and d escription 
The 3- pane windows can influence the distortion and color from interior camera images. Information is 
known on each pane individually, but not how they act as a set. The objective of the Window Optical 
Quality DFTO is to configure the Nikon D5 camera to take pictures out of the forward windows and the 
docking hatch window to determine optical quality and the best window to use during the lunar flyby. No 
changes to the planned configuration for flyby observations were made post - Window Quality DFTO. The 
analysis reveals Window 2 as the best window for imaging, and we are Go for collecting images at 400 mm 
focal lengths. The spacecraft was pointed to put the Moon in Wind ow 2 and imaging should be completed 
from the left position. 
The docking hatch window is optically superior to the forward windows, but completing operations using 
the docking hatch window would limit observations to one crew member. Therefore, we would still prefer 
to use the forward windows for observations but hope we can get images through the docking hatch window 
on lunar approach. 
We recommend providing reminders to the crew to keep the lens flush with the window as much as possible 
to prevent imaging through multiple windowpanes at an angle. If an image must be acquired at an angle, 
the recommendation is to limit the maximum focal length to 200 mm or to acquire the image at multiple 
focal lengths. 
7.5.2. Supporting information 
For the analysis of the Optical Quality DFTO, the target was the Earth. The distinctly visible clouds were 
used to look for textures and hard contrast shifts. These cloud structures enabled the comparison of the 
images collected in different windows, at different positions, and with different focal lengths. Assessments 
were made through overlapping and side -by-side comparisons of images. 
7.5.3. Selection of focal length 
Images were collected with the Nikon D5 camera fitted with an 80 -400 mm zoom lens set to focal lengths 
values of 80, 135, 200, and 400 mm. We focused on the comparison between the 200 mm and 400 mm 
focal lengths due to the greater sensitivity of the longer focal lengths to the optical characteristics of the 
windows. 
Most images collected at 200 mm are clearer than those collected at 400 mm despite the increase in 
magnification. This is counter to expectations in the absence of distortion, where 400 mm images would 
resolve more detail than shorter focal lengths. For Window 3 at all positions (Left, Center, and Right), the 
200 mm images were substantially clearer and resolved more detail than the 400 mm images (Figure 11). 
However, the 400 mm images were clearer and resolved more detail than the 200 mm images in the Window 
2 Left position (Figure 10). For the Docking Hatch Window, the 400 mm image provides the most detail 
over the 200 mm image (Figure 12). 
7.5.4. Selection of window 
While images through the Docking Hatch Window at all focal lengths show little to no degradation, this 
window will not allow multiple crew members to simultaneously view the Moon during the lunar flyby. 
Therefore, the decision was made to select between Window 2 and Window 3. Following the analysis of 
images and .wav files collected in FD03 by pilot Victor Glover, the lunar science team has chosen Window 
2 for imaging during the lunar flyby. Within Window 2, images to left of center were the clearest. Thus,
